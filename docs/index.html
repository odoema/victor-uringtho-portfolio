TRK 01 · The Story

A Journey of Faith, Passion and Music

Each chapter is marked with a chord Victor learned — in the order he learned them.

C
CHAPTER 01
1973

Show Us Our Home

Born in Kamuli to a nurse and a haematologist; an Alur son of Busoga.

I was born on 31 May 1973 in Kamuli, Uganda, to the late Edith Basirika Uringtho and Fred Binego Uringtho. At the time of my birth, my father was in Nigeria pursuing further studies. My mother, a trained nurse, travelled from Kampala to her hometown of Kamuli so that she could receive support from her family during childbirth.

I am an Alur by heritage, born in Busoga. My name, Unyuthfua, means “Show us our home” in the Alur language. I am the firstborn of my parents, and I have two brothers and two sisters.

Little did I know that the passion that would shape much of my life was already taking root during my childhood.

“My name, Unyuthfua, means ‘Show us our home.’”
G7
CHAPTER 02
Childhood

The Boy Who Made His Own Guitar

A Sunday-school guitarist, no guitar of his own — so he built one.

My musical journey began at a very early age when I attended Sunday school. There was a young man who played the guitar and sang songs, and I was fascinated by what he could do with the instrument. I wanted to play just like him.

There was only one problem: I did not have a guitar, and my parents could not afford to buy me one at the time. But the desire to make music was stronger than the limitation.

I carved a flat arm from a piece of wood to serve as the neck and attached it to an old pan, which became its sound box. I then used wires from a motorcycle clutch cable as my strings — not comfortable, but it didn’t matter. I would happily play it for anyone willing to listen, including the young man whose playing had inspired me. I later discovered that he was actually my cousin.

“When you have a passion for something, you find a way to begin, even when you do not have everything you need.”
F
CHAPTER 03
1986

First Chords in Nairobi

A cousin, a borrowed guitar and a week of Christmas holidays.

After completing Primary Seven, I joined my family in Nairobi, where my father was working with Aga Khan Hospital. During the Christmas holidays, my cousin Stephen Kasamba visited from Kampala, bringing along a guitar.

Stephen taught me my first two chords: C and G7. I spent the entire day practising them. When he returned from town later that day, he taught me a third chord, F. By the following day, I could play all the songs I knew using those three chords. He then introduced me to D, and before long I could play the songs I knew in two keys, C and G.

Stephen stayed for only a week. When he left, I was once again without a guitar — and I would wait another two years before I could play one again.

“By the following day, I could play all the songs I knew using those three chords.”
D
CHAPTER 04
c. 1988

A Guitar of My Own

A nylon-stringed gift from Germany — and the fastest student his teacher had seen.

That opportunity came through Stephen’s younger brother, Peter Kasamba — the very guitarist I had admired during Sunday school. Peter visited us in Nairobi with a nylon-stringed guitar. This time something remarkable happened: the guitar was mine. Stephen, who had travelled to Germany, had bought it for me as a gift.

I was told I was the first student he had taught who learned to play so quickly — two days, to be precise. Peter taught me more chords, including A, Am, B, Bm, E, Em and Dm. When he returned to Kampala, I finally had an instrument of my own.

School commitments meant that most of my playing happened during the holidays, but my passion continued to grow.

“Something remarkable happened: the guitar was mine.”
A
CHAPTER 05
1991 – 1995

Growing Through Church and Fellowship

Keyboard, then drums and bass — learning by serving.

My interest in music gradually expanded beyond the guitar. I became interested in the keyboard, and another cousin, Patrick Kasamba, taught me what he knew about playing it. In return, I shared my guitar knowledge with him — a wonderful exchange of skills.

Eventually, I began playing guitar and piano during home fellowship meetings and church services. At times, we were the instrumentalists available, and that gave me opportunities to practise, serve and improve.

Between 1991 and 1993 I kept developing. Through Senior Five and Senior Six, from 1993 to 1995, my skills grew considerably; I learned more guitar and keyboard and went on to explore the drums and bass guitar. I was not simply learning how to play — I was developing an appreciation of rhythm, harmony, arrangement, and the way instruments work together.

“At times, we were the instrumentalists available — and that gave me opportunities to practise, serve and improve.”
Am
CHAPTER 06
1995

The Anglican Youth Fellowship Band

A turning point: music as teamwork.

A significant turning point came in 1995 when I joined the Anglican Youth Fellowship band. My cousin Peter Kasamba, my first guitar teacher and an important mentor, was part of the team. It was also during this period that I met Arthur Mutesasira, who would later play an important role in shaping and strengthening my keyboard playing.

The experience helped me understand that music was more than an individual pursuit. It was also about teamwork, learning from others, sharing ideas, and continually refining one’s craft.

My curiosity was beginning to extend beyond performing. I wanted to understand how music was put together, how instrumental sounds were created, and how a musical idea could become a finished recording.

“Music was more than an individual pursuit.”
Em
CHAPTER 07
c. 2000

Four Tracks in Wandegeya

A Roland XP-80, a four-track tape recorder and a first master copy.

My first recording project, as I recall it, took place around the year 2000. It was for a gentleman named Zephaniah Mukalere, who believed I could produce his project. I was still developing my skills and working with limited equipment, but I accepted the challenge.

I worked from a small studio in Wandegeya, Kampala, using a Roland XP-80 to create the soundtracks. I recorded his vocals on a four-track tape recorder, mixed the material to the best of my ability and produced my first master copy.

Zephaniah later travelled to the United Kingdom, taking the master with him. He returned with a Korg N264 workstation and an Akai DPS16 Digital Multi-track Recorder — sixteen tracks, eight channels of simultaneous recording. It opened up new possibilities. There was no looking back.

“I had moved from playing music to helping create recorded music from start to finish.”
Dm
CHAPTER 08
2002 – today

“Thank You Lord” — and Beyond

His own album, study in the UK, and a working life in studios and on stages.

In those early days I was not yet widely known as someone who recorded and produced music, and clients were few. Rather than let that discourage me, I invested my time in creating my own music. In 2002, I released my album, Thank You Lord. Since then, I have recorded and worked on numerous projects for different artists. I have also travelled to the United Kingdom to study rhythm programming, music recording and production. Today I write music, play instruments, arrange compositions, create soundtracks, record, mix and master. I provide live sound mixing and recording, and I mix and master for artists who send me their studio stems from outside Uganda.

“Every project has taught me something, and every new artist has presented a different musical idea, challenge, and opportunity.”

The Legacy

Helping others realise their dreams

Victor has spent his working life on the other side of the glass — building soundtracks, recording first-time singers, mixing bands for festival crowds, and finishing stems sent from abroad. His contribution is measured less in his own name than in the records and performances of the people he has worked with.

“A music producer’s role goes beyond operating equipment, arranging instruments, or adjusting levels on a mixing console. It also involves listening, understanding an artist’s vision, offering guidance, and helping that artist bring out the best in their work.”

“Sometimes, what an artist needs most is someone who believes in their idea, recognises its potential, and is willing to work patiently with them until the music begins to take shape. That is a responsibility I take seriously.”

“Music is not merely what I do. It is a lifelong journey, a calling, and a way of serving others through creativity.”Victor Unyuthfua Uringtho
